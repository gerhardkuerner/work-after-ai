# The framework of the report, verbatim from the 2026 edition (version 1.3)

Two chapters of the report carry its framework: the distinction between measured, assessed and unknown, and the separation of the three forces. They stand here unchanged, so that an agent works with the same terms as the report.

## What a trend is and what it is not, and what this report does not know

This report distinguishes three things that constantly run into one another in the discussion about artificial intelligence and work: what is measured, what is assessed and what nobody can know. Measured are the three forces around which the main part turns. The AI argument can be shown in layoff statistics, company surveys and capital market communication, the career gap in payroll and register data, the long rebuild in surveys, official statistics and by now in the usage data of the model vendors themselves. These three forces are trends in the strict sense, because they have a direction that appears in several independent sources and over more than one year, and because it can be checked in the coming year whether they hold. Assessed are the predictions and the outlooks at the end of the chapters; they state the author's view, are derived from the documented findings and are marked in the text as assessments. Nobody can know how fast the technology itself gets better. This report makes no statement about when a model reaches which capability. It cites the scenarios of others as benchmarks and grades them, but it does not place a scenario of its own beside them. Its central thesis does not depend on that abstention, because it holds that the rebuild of work is for now not failing because of the machine but because of the organization, and that applies to every speed of technical progress that is plausible in 2026.

What this report does not know belongs on this page as well. It does not know whether it means protection or delay that no effect has so far shown up at the entry level in Europe, because the spread of the technology here lags the American one by two to three years, and the measurements that could settle this question will not exist before 2027. It does not know whether the gap between pilot and production is a transitional phenomenon that closes as organizations learn, or whether, as the Notion survey suggests, it opens further with growing maturity. It does not know how the effect looks in firms with fewer than ten employees, because there is no reliable survey for that in Europe, and it knows next to nothing about wages, training and codetermination in small firms. It does not know what share of the American layoffs the technology actually accounts for, because the statistics count the reason the company states, not the one that applies. And it does not know whether the usage data of the model vendors, which it treats in several places as the sharpest measurement so far, will still count as such in five years, because none of these data series can be recalculated from outside. These gaps are not weaknesses of the report that could have been avoided, but the state of knowledge in September 2026, and they are the reason the report appears annually.

## Three forces in the labor market, one wrong headline, and the question that really counts

---

### The headline that tries to explain too much at once

Hardly any story has taken hold as effortlessly over the past year as the one about artificial intelligence pushing people out of work. It has the advantage that it appears to confirm itself the moment you open the news. One large technology company announces thousands of job cuts on a single day, another eliminates entire departments, and in almost every one of these statements the word appears that promises to give the whole thing its meaning. The technology takes over, the human steps aside, and the layoff statistics deliver the evidence along with it.

The story is catchy, and in this form it is wrong. Not because artificial intelligence leaves no traces in the labor market, but because the headline fuses three completely different processes, which in truth rest on different causes and run on different time scales, into a single story. Anyone who takes it apart sees a picture that is less dramatic but far more consequential than the one in the headline. And they see where the question that actually matters lies, the one that is almost entirely lost in the excitement about the machine.

### The first force is the AI argument, a wave of layoffs that calls itself artificial intelligence and goes by another name

The most visible of the three forces is the wave of job cuts, and it is at the same time the one that has the least to do with the technology in whose name it happens. A survey by the consulting firm Gartner (gartner.com) among 350 large companies, all of which already work with artificial intelligence, did find that four out of five of these companies have cut staff. But it also showed that there is no connection between the depth of the cuts and the economic success of the investments. Those who cut the most did not end up better off, and in some cases worse. That is a remarkable finding, because it contradicts the internal logic of the headline. If the job cuts were really the result of a more productive machine, they would have to show up in the numbers, and that is exactly what they do not do. [18]

What shows up instead is an older and more sober pattern. A substantial part of what is presented today as technological modernization is the correction of overstaffing that built up in the years of cheap money. Investors from Silicon Valley, who are hardly suspected of underestimating the importance of the technology, say openly that nearly every large company is overstaffed, many by a quarter, some by half and more. [19] Artificial intelligence supplies the better argument for these cuts, because a reduction presented as modernization lands differently in the capital markets than a plain cost saving decision. This first force is therefore real in its consequences for the people affected, but it is wrongly named in its cause. This report therefore gives it the name that describes its mechanics, the AI argument.

### The second force, the career gap, hits those starting out, and its cause is disputed in the research

The second force is subtler and more serious. It shows up not in the headlines about individual corporations, but in the data on young people entering working life. A widely noted study by the Stanford Digital Economy Lab showed, on the basis of actual payroll data, that employment among 22 to 25 year olds in those occupations that are particularly exposed to artificial intelligence has declined markedly in comparison, while older and more experienced employees in the same occupations remained stable or even gained. [4] Here, then, the technology appears to be doing exactly what the headline claims.

But at this point the matter becomes harder rather than simpler, because the research is anything but united about the cause. A study from the Federal Reserve by Jessica Liu and Douglas Webber covering more than a million companies finds no measurable effect,[20] and an analysis by the payments provider Ramp (ramp.com) together with the labor market data provider Revelio Labs (reveliolabs.com) does confirm the collapse at the entry level, but shows that it is precisely not driven by those firms that are actually adopting artificial intelligence.[21] A Stanford study of companies in 41 countries, by contrast, concludes that the entry point narrows precisely at the adopting companies, though through growth among the experienced and not through cuts among the young.[143] The collapse is therefore demonstrable, but attributing it to the technology is contested, and a substantial part of it is likely to go back to the sharpest turn in interest rates in four decades, which made every new hire at the entry level more expensive overnight. Anyone who wants to treat this second force honestly has to endure the dispute instead of resolving it in favor of the simple story. Because its finding appears in the same place regardless of how that dispute ends, namely at the beginning of the working life, this report carries it under the name of the career gap.

### The third force, the long rebuild, is the actual effect of the technology, and it is slower than its reputation

The third force is the real change that artificial intelligence sets off inside companies, and it has the least in common with the pace of the headlines. It does not run through the spectacular replacement of entire workforces, but through the slow and laborious reworking of processes, through the question of which task a digital team member can take over and which it cannot, and through the far harder question of how an organization builds the necessary knowledge in the first place. This force is the most consequential of the three, but it is invisible, because it cannot be condensed into a press release. It happens in the engine rooms of companies, not on their front pages. This report calls it the long rebuild, because what decides success is not the new machine but the patient reorganization of work around the machine.

### Why the three forces are constantly confused

The fusing of the three into a single story is not ill will but an understandable confusion, because all three are happening at the same time. The AI argument, the career gap and the long rebuild fall into the same window of time, and the human need to link simultaneous events causally does the rest. Artificial intelligence is at the center of attention anyway, and so it becomes the obvious explanation for everything that is moving in the labor market at once. The analytical work does not consist in denying one of these three forces, but in separating them cleanly from one another and showing the real mechanics of each.

This separation is the subject of the present study. It rests on the publicly available quantitative research, which it reports honestly in its contradictions instead of picking out the convenient findings. It places an emphasis on the European labor market, which is a blind spot in the largely American discussion, even though its structures differ considerably. And it uses only numbers that have been checked against their primary source and, where one exists, against a second independent source. Only with this separation and this checking does it become possible to see behind the headline.

### What this is really about in the end

Once the three forces have been separated, the question that really counts shifts to an unexpected place. It is then no longer whether artificial intelligence destroys jobs, because that question, as will become clear, is posed wrongly. It is how fast an organization builds the knowledge that makes the technology productive in the first place, and whether, in the haste of cutting jobs, it is destroying precisely the capability that will later decide its success. This question stands at the end of the study, not at its beginning. The path there leads through the three forces, and it begins with the one that is the loudest and delivers the least of what its name promises.
